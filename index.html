I am a labor economist and postdoctoral fellow at the FAIR Center of Excellence at the Norwegian School of Economics (NHH), and a research affiliate at RFBerlin.

My research studies how public policies, educational institutions, and information about job characteristics shape labor market outcomes and inequality, with particular attention to inequality across socioeconomic backgrounds and genders.

My work combines rich administrative data with surveys and information extracted from text.

I am on the 2026–2027 academic job market.

Job Market Paper
Social Assistance, Work Incentives, and Economic Hardship
Jason Cook Chloe N. East Adam Reiremo Alexander Willén

Social assistance is the last line of defense in the welfare state, providing support to individuals who cannot meet their basic needs through employment or other resources. Its central challenge is to protect recipients against severe economic hardship without discouraging employment among those capable of achieving economic self-sufficiency. Using quasi-random assignment of applicants to caseworkers in Norway, we distinguish between generosity in benefit amount and duration. We find that the two margins have very different consequences: higher benefit amounts cause temporary employment losses, while longer duration persistently reduces employment but protects against severe hardship.

Figure 1: Effects of a Higher Amount and Longer Duration

Notes. Estimated effects of a $400 (2012 USD) higher monthly benefit (amount, blue) or 10 additional days of receipt (duration, orange), relative to the year before application. Both are associated with about $1,000 in additional social assistance in the application year, including subsequent applications. Causal effects are estimated using a caseworker leniency IV design. Employment: labor income above 1.5 times the National Insurance reference amount (1.5G, ~$21,000 in 2012 USD). Severe poverty: household income below the 10th percentile of pre-application household income. Bars show 95% confidence intervals. "Amount − duration" reports the difference between the amount and duration estimates.
Significance levels: * p < 0.10, ** p < 0.05, *** p < 0.01.

Figure 1 documents that a higher monthly benefit causes a temporary drop in employment, and protects against severe poverty in the application year. Longer duration instead persistently reduces employment, but protects against severe poverty in the following years. In the paper, we find that longer duration lowers average household income, suggesting that longer duration protects households at the bottom of the income distribution. We also find that reductions in debt and mortality are concentrated among recipients with weaker labor market attachment, while employment losses are largest among those with stronger attachment.
