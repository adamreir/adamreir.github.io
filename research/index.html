Job Market Paper
Social Assistance, Work Incentives, and Economic Hardship (with Jason Cook, Chloe N. East, and Alexander L. P. Willén)
Social assistance is the last line of defense in the welfare state, providing support to individuals who cannot meet their basic needs through employment or other resources. Its central challenge is to protect recipients against severe economic hardship without discouraging employment among those capable of achieving economic self-sufficiency. We study this tradeoff using quasi-random assignment of applicants to caseworkers in Norway. We distinguish between generosity in benefit amount and duration and examine their effects on employment and economic hardship. Higher benefit amounts generate temporary employment losses, with some evidence of longer-run gains, but provide relatively limited protection against severe hardship. Longer duration, by contrast, persistently reduces employment and earnings while providing greater protection against very low income, debt, and mortality. Moreover, the employment costs and protective benefits of longer duration are concentrated among different recipients: employment losses are largest among those with stronger prior labor market attachment, who have greater scope to return to work, while reductions in debt and mortality are concentrated among those with weaker attachment and fewer employment alternatives. Our findings show that evaluating social assistance requires considering both its employment costs and its protective benefits, which depend critically on how generosity is provided and who receives it.

Reproducibility and Robustness of Economics and Political Science Research (with Abel Brodeur and 350 co-authors)
Published in Nature (2026)
Science aspires to be cumulative. Reproducibility efforts strengthen science by testing the reliability of published findings, promoting self-correction, and informing policy-making. Computational reproductions, whereby independent researchers reproduce the results of published studies, are an essential diagnostic tool. Such efforts should have greater visibility. However, little social science reproduction and robustness has been conducted at scale. Here we reproduced original analyses and conducted robustness checks of 110 articles that were published in leading economics and political science journals with mandatory data and code sharing policies. We found that more than 85% of published claims were computationally reproducible. In robustness checks, our reanalyses showed that 72% of statistically significant estimates remain significant and in the same direction, and the median reproduced effect size is nearly the same as the originally published effect size (that is, 99% of the published effect size). Additionally, 6 independent research teams examined 12 pre-specified hypotheses about determinants of robustness. Research teams with more experience found lower levels of robustness, and robustness did not correlate with author characteristics or data availability.

The Pay and Non-Pay Content of Job Ads (with Richard Audoly and Manudeep Bhuller)
Revise & Resubmit (second round) · American Economic Review
We shed light on the pay and non-pay content of job ads using a comprehensive database of vacancies posted by Norwegian employers. We develop a methodology to systematically classify information on pay and non-pay job attributes that are publicly advertised in vacancy texts, and combine this with a revealed preference approach to estimate workers' valuation of employers from observed pay and job-to-job flows. This allows us to study the types of job attributes offered by good employers and quantify how well employer pay premiums and amenities can be predicted by job attributes retrieved from vacancy texts. About 55% of job ads provide some pay-related information, while nearly all ads feature some information on non-pay attributes. High pay employers mention pay-related attributes more often, while high amenity employers mention flexible working hours and permanent contracts more. We show that publicly advertised job attributes are meaningful predictors of employer ranks, and that non-pay attributes are about as predictive as pay-related attributes. In light of recent policy proposals aimed at improving pay transparency in job postings, our quantitative evidence suggests that publicly advertised information on both pay and non-pay attributes can have implications for the strength and direction of job-to-job flows.

Field of Study and Comparative Advantage (single authored)
Working paper on request
Do students' relative academic strengths shape their returns to different fields of study? Using admission thresholds in Norway's centralized college admission system, this paper examines how returns to STEM programs vary with students' academic backgrounds. Students with stronger mathematics grades, particularly relative to their language grades, have higher returns to STEM. However, prioritizing students based on these relative strengths would reduce the representation of women and students from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, highlighting a tradeoff between efficiency and equity in college admissions.

Career Effects of Female Board Recruitment: Evidence from the Norwegian Board Quotas (with Lisa Kahn and Alexander L. P. Willén)
Do corporate board appointments facilitate career advancement among women? We study Norway's board quota reform, which required publicly listed companies to have at least 40% representation of each gender on their boards. Using administrative register data and variation in firms' exposure to the reform, we examine how board appointments affect earnings, employment, and career progression.
College Choice, Gender Bias, and Large Language Models (with Catalina Franco and Florentine Oliveira-Roux)
Does the growing use of AI in educational guidance risk reinforcing gender segregation across fields of study? This project investigates whether LLM-powered chatbots provide systematically different educational recommendations to male and female students, and whether such differences affect subsequent college applications. Using experimental variation and administrative records on college applications, we examine how AI-generated advice shapes educational choices.
